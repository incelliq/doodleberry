## Open questions
- f1: (Non-blocking) Ravi's line ends 'so blue reaches us from every direction'. The page doesn't say this. It says blue light 'is scattered in all directions' and 'This is why we see a blue sky most of the time.' The line's ending is a fair, true inference (light scattered in all directions reaches the viewer from all over the sky), but it is a paraphrase, not the page's words. It also differs from the claim, which ends 'so the sky looks blue'. And the quote leaves out the sentence that supports the claim's 'so the sky looks blue'. Suggested: Add 'This is why we see a blue sky most of the time.' to the f1 quote (it comes straight after the quoted sentences). Then either change the claim to match the line (e.g. '...scattered in all directions, so blue light reaches us from all over the sky') or change the line to stay closer to the page, e.g. 'Air scatters blue light in all directions, more than reds and yellows. That's why we see a blue sky.'
- f2: (Non-blocking) Ravi says the light 'crosses much more air'. The page says 'passing through more of the atmosphere', so the intensifier 'much' isn't on the page. It is physically true (light from a Sun on the horizon crosses many times more air than light from overhead), but strictly it can't be traced to the source. Suggested: Use 'At sunset the light crosses more air.' (or 'passes through more air'), or add a source that supports the degree.

Ravi's line only says 'more than reds and yellows', which is true, so the spoken line is fine. The claim text is broader than it needs to be. Suggested: Optionally narrow the claim to 'more than reds and yellows' so it matches the line and avoids the violet objection. The quote can stay as it is.

## Counter-evidence
- Violet light: violet has shorter waves than blue and is scattered even more, so a careful reader may ask why the sky isn't violet. Common explanations: sunlight contains less violet than blue, some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and human eyes are less sensitive to violet. (These are general background, not from the cited page.) Ravi's line only compares blue with 'reds and yellows' and never says blue is scattered most, so it stays true. The f1 claim and the page's wording ('more than other colors') are what the objection would hit.
- 'From every direction': the page notes that 'Closer to the horizon, the sky fades to a lighter blue or white' because of repeated scattering and reflection from the ground. Blue skylight still arrives from all over the sky, so the line stays true, but the sky is not equally blue everywhere. The line doesn't claim it is.
- What does the scattering: the page's short answer credits 'all the gases and particles in the air', while the body text (and the f1 quote) credits 'the tiny molecules of air'. The blue of a clear sky comes mostly from scattering by gas molecules, so 'Air scatters blue light' is a fair simplification for children.
- Sunset colours: the page adds that dust, pollution and other aerosols also scatter blue light and can make the sky glow red. Ravi gives only the path-length explanation. That explanation is true and is the page's main one, so leaving aerosols out is a simplification, not an error. Sunsets also aren't always orange; the line doesn't say they are.
- 'Scattered away': the page says 'Even more of the blue light is scattered'. 'Away' (out of the direct path to the viewer) is implied by the page's next clause, 'allowing the reds and yellows to pass straight through to your eyes', and is physically correct. Some of that scattered blue still lights other parts of the sky at sunset, which the line doesn't deny.
- 'Much more air' at sunset is physically correct. The path through the atmosphere near the horizon is many times longer than straight overhead. Only the size of that difference is missing from the page.
